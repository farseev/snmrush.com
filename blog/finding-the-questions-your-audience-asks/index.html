Finding the questions your audience actually asks

10 Aug 2026 · 6 min read · SNMRush editorial team · FAQ

The questions your audience asks are best found in their own conversations, not only in keyword tools. Combine search data with social listening, community threads, sales calls and support tickets, group what you hear into tensions, then write one clear page per question. AI assistants answer conversational prompts, so conversational research fits them.

Why are keyword lists no longer enough?

Keyword research was built for a search box where people typed three or four words. Prompts to AI assistants are longer and more personal: "I run a small skincare brand in Singapore and my content sounds like everyone else's, what should I do?" That question contains a category, a location, a problem and an emotion. A keyword tool reduces it to "skincare content ideas".

Keyword data is still useful. It tells you about demand and how people phrase short queries. But it rarely reveals the tension behind a search, and tension is what decides which answer feels useful.

Where do the real questions live?

  • Public social conversation: posts, comments and threads where people complain, compare and ask each other for advice.
  • Communities and forums: long-form questions with context, and answers others endorse.
  • Sales and support: the objections and confusions your team hears every week.
  • Search data: People Also Ask, autocomplete and Search Console queries, especially long ones.
  • AI answers themselves: ask assistants about your category and note the follow-up questions they suggest.

How does SNMRush turn conversation into a question map?

We use SOMIN, an AI audience-research platform, as our technology partner for this step. SOMIN analyses real social posts and surfaces concepts: the tones, emotions and tensions people express, grouped into themes. The crucial feature for us is traceability. Every concept links back to the posts that evidence it, so we can check that a theme is real and read the exact words people use. The SOMIN AI agents page describes how its research agents work.

That matters for a reason beyond accuracy. Our listening across marketing conversations keeps returning to one theme: people distrust brands that claim to know them too well, and trust the ones that listen. Building content from what people actually said, rather than from what an algorithm inferred about them, is a small, practical way to honour that.

The process runs in four steps:

  1. Gather. Pull conversation about the category, competitors and adjacent problems over the last six to twelve months.
  2. Cluster. Group posts into tensions: the underlying conflict, such as "I need more content but I am afraid it will sound generic".
  3. Phrase. For each tension, write the questions people ask, in their wording, from beginner to expert.
  4. Map. Match each question to an existing page, a page to rewrite, or a page to create.

Definitions

  • Tension: a conflict or pain point people feel, which produces many related questions.
  • Question map: a structured list of audience questions, each assigned to one best page.
  • Conversational query: a long, natural-language question of the kind typed into AI assistants.

A worked example

Take marketing leaders exploring AI. A keyword tool shows demand for "AI marketing tools" and "AI content generator". Listening reveals more: worry that AI will replace their role, frustration with AI content that needs heavy rewriting, anxiety about explaining results to a board, and a desire to find peers who are figuring it out too. Each becomes a cluster of questions. "Will AI replace marketing managers?" "Why does AI content still need so much editing?" "How do I report AI visibility to my CEO?"

Notice that some of these are not product questions at all. They are human ones. That is precisely why communities like Marketing Mondays, the Singapore marketing community and weekly show founded by SOMIN, exist: one real signal, decoded and made actionable, discussed with peers. For our clients, those human questions are often where an honest, useful page earns the most trust and the most citations.

How do you prioritise the questions?

Score each question on three factors: how often it appears in conversation, how close it is to a buying decision, and how poorly it is currently answered by search results and AI assistants. The best opportunities are frequent, close to decision, and badly served. Persona work helps too; our sister consultancy Mindfuse builds personas and tension maps for Southeast Asian audiences, and the same thinking sharpens prioritisation.

Then write the pages using answer-first structure, and add the questions to your AI visibility prompt panel so you can see whether engines start citing you.

What does this look like in practice?

The SOMIN case library has several examples of brands and agencies using conversation evidence to shape what they say. The SM Universe case study is a good place to start for how audience signals become content direction.

What should you avoid?

  • Treating volume as importance. A loud complaint from a small group may not represent buyers.
  • Paraphrasing away the language. Keep the audience's words; they are what people type into assistants.
  • Writing one page per phrasing. Several phrasings of the same question belong on one strong page.
  • Forgetting to recheck. Conversations move, and last year's tension may have faded.

Finally, share the question map beyond marketing. Sales, product and support teams recognise these questions instantly, and they often add the ones listening missed. A map that the whole company trusts is far more likely to be kept current.

The shift is simple to describe and hard to fake. Stop guessing what people want to know. Listen, write down what they actually ask, and answer it better than anyone else.

Frequently asked questions

Is keyword research dead?

No. It still shows demand and phrasing for short queries. It works best combined with conversational research that reveals the tension and context behind a question.

What is a question map?

A structured list of real audience questions, grouped by tension and stage, with each question assigned to the single best page that answers it.

How often should the question map be refreshed?

Quarterly for most categories, or sooner if a new product, regulation or news event changes what people are asking.
